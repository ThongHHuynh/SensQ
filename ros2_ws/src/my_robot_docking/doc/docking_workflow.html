Corridor Docking

01

What talks to what

Three nodes and one arbiter. The detector turns camera frames into tag detections and TF; the docking server fuses those with odometry and the laser scan into velocity commands; the arbiter decides whether the base listens to docking or to Nav2.

02

The dock action, start to finish

One goal, one dock. The server rejects a second goal while it is busy. Everything after the optional Nav2 leg runs inside a retry loop, so a lost tag costs an attempt rather than the whole goal.

03

Everything is measured on the axis

The tag has an outward normal. Extend it into the room and you get the approach axis — the line the robot must travel to arrive square. Two points sit on it: the docked pose at final_distance from the tag, and the corridor entry further out at predocking_distance.

Errors are resolved onto that axis rather than measured as a bearing to the goal. This is the single most important idea in the controller, and the thing the previous version got wrong.

The distinction that matters: cross does not grow with distance. A bearing to the goal does. Two robots 5 cm off the line, one at 0.6 m and one at 2 m out, have the same cross-track error and need the same correction — but wildly different bearings.

Backing in reuses all of it. Only the target heading changes: the robot holds axis + π and drives negative, so its rear leads down the same corridor.

04

Why angled approaches used to fail

The old controller steered at the docked pose and summed three angular terms. It also declared an overshoot whenever the along-axis error went negative. For a robot beside the dock, that error is negative from the very first tick — being level with the tag puts you behind the docked pose's plane. The goal aborted before the wheels turned.

Overshoot is now a run-phase fact

A negative along only means something once the robot is on the axis and aligned. The abort therefore requires all three: along < -overshoot_margin, |cross| ≤ corridor_half_width, and |yaw| ≤ entry_yaw_tolerance. Starting behind the plane is geometry, not a fault.

05

The corridor state machine

Five phases. Every one is either a rotation in place or a straight line, which is what makes the swept area predictable — a controller that only ever turns or only ever goes straight cannot arc into the dock.

Which phase the approach starts in depends on where the robot already is. Being off the corridor needs repositioning; a pure heading error needs only a turn.

Phase behaviour
PhaseMotionGuidanceTag needed
ENTER_TURN Rotate in place Odometry, toward the latched waypoint No
ENTER_DRIVE Straight line Odometry, bearing hold to the waypoint No
ALIGN Rotate in place Odometry; needs the tag to exit To leave
RUN Straight down the axis Visual servo, re-anchored every tick Yes, unless reversing
REACHED Stopped Re-checks tolerances for verification_duration Same as RUN

The run law

A Stanley-style law: hold the axis heading, and close the perpendicular deviation with a term that softens as speed rises. 

speed = clamp(distance_kp * along, min_linear_speed, max_linear_speed)
omega = yaw_kp * yaw + atan2(cross_track_kp * cross, speed + 0.05)

There is no bearing-to-goal term, which is why the law stays well behaved as the goal distance goes to zero — a bearing swings through ±π on millimetre noise when you are sitting on top of the point you are measuring to.

Backing in needs no sign flip on the cross-track term. Reversing mirrors the travelling frame's left against the axis and moves the leading point from the nose to the rear; the two cancel.

Turning in place

A half turn puts the yaw error on the ±π branch cut, where its sign flips on noise and a naive controller reverses direction forever. The turn direction is latched on entry and only re-evaluated once the error is comfortably off the cut, below π/2.

06

Anchoring, and the field-of-view problem

The camera sees roughly ±40°. Any manoeuvre that puts the robot onto the axis from off to one side must turn the tag out of frame — that is unavoidable, not a bug to fix.

So the controller does not servo on live pixels. It latches the tag pose into the odom frame and plans against that, refreshing the latch whenever the tag comes back into view. Entry legs run on odometry by design; the run phase is visual.

  • The first anchor averages anchor_samples observations and drops the single worst outlier — one bad detection tilts the whole axis, and the axis is what the entry legs are planned from.
  • Later observations are low-passed onto the latch with anchor_filter_alpha, so angular noise does not swing the corridor around under the robot.
  • Backing in is odometry-only for its last stretch, bounded by max_reverse_distance. The entry distance for a reverse dock is staging_distance rather than predocking_distance, precisely to keep that blind leg short.
07

Safety layers

What can stop an approach
GuardWhen it appliesNote
Sensor freshness Every tick Odometry and scan must be newer than sensor_timeout
Minimum tag distance Every tick The last guard that stays live inside the dock face
Swept rotation clearance Rotating in place Closest return in any direction, excluding the dock's own bearing
Directional stop Moving, outside the contact band Front sector when driving, rear sector when backing
Reverse travel budget Backing in Bounds how far odometry alone is trusted

Two of these need explaining, because the obvious implementation of each is wrong.

Rotation clearance excludes the dock. A rotation sweeps the whole footprint, so every bearing matters — except the station the robot is deliberately parked in front of, which would otherwise dominate the minimum and veto every turn near a dock.

The directional stop mutes at the dock face. Inside final_distance + dock_contact_distance the dock fills the very sector the robot is driving into, so the stop would fault on its own target. Below that band, minimum_tag_distance is the guard that remains.

Stop distances are measured from base_footprint

The circumscribed footprint radius is about 0.17 m — the camera nose sits at 0.163 m. A stop distance below that only fires once the robot has already made contact, which is why the front and rear stops sit at 0.22 m.

Reading the logs

The server logs one line on every phase change, which is usually enough to tell a control problem from a perception one. 

Docking home_dock (forward): enter_turn along=-0.300 cross=-1.200 yaw=1.571
Docking home_dock (forward): enter_drive along=-0.300 cross=-1.200 yaw=1.001
Docking home_dock (forward): align      along= 0.700 cross=-0.021 yaw=1.729
Docking home_dock (forward): run        along= 0.700 cross=-0.021 yaw=0.048
Docking home_dock (forward): reached    along= 0.002 cross= 0.004 yaw=0.019
  • Never leaves enter_drive — the waypoint is unreachable, or odometry is drifting faster than the leg completes.
  • Cycles run → enter_turn — cross-track is blowing out. Check the tag normal sign and the anchor filter before touching gains.
  • Sits in align — aligned but no fresh tag, so the run will not start. A perception problem, not a control one.
  • cross large and constant — the axis itself is wrong. Suspect the dock database, not the controller.
09

Dock database and the two yaw conventions

A dock definition is a tag id, a reference pose, and three distances that must satisfy predocking > staging > final.

reference_pose yaw means two different things

A dock written by record_dock_pose stores the raw tag frame pose. Its yaw lies in the tag plane, so the approach direction is derived from reference_pose minus staging_pose instead, and reference_pose[2] is ignored.

A hand-authored dock using staging_distance has no robot pose to derive from, so there reference_pose[2] is the approach direction.

The two differ by about a quarter turn. Copying yaw values between the two forms aims the dock sideways, so the database reports the disagreement at load time rather than on the floor. 

[WARN] Dock home_dock: approach direction 3.116 rad derived from staging_pose
       differs from reference_pose yaw 1.577 rad by 1.539 rad.
10

What the tests actually prove

The controller is free of ROS types, so the whole state machine runs under plain pytest. The convergence suite is the one worth knowing about: it rolls a unicycle model forward under the controller's own commands from seven start angles — 0°, 45°, 90°, 90° mirrored, 135°, 180°, and an offset skew — forwards and in reverse.

Alongside convergence it asserts something the unit tests cannot: that no pose in any executed path is both near the dock and off the axis. That is the hugging failure stated as a property, and it is what a per-tick test would miss. 

cd src/my_robot_docking && python3 -m pytest test -q

A law that looks correct one tick at a time can still orbit, stall, or graze the dock over a hundred of them. Both bugs found late in this work — the inverted reverse cross-track sign, and the residual yaw error that no amount of steering could remove once the robot had stopped — were caught by the simulation, not by inspection.
